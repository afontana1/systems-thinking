# EN.645.780 — Agile Systems Engineering

**Credits:** 3  
**Prerequisite:** EN.645.662 Introduction to Systems Engineering  
**Recommended self-study preparation:** completion of Phases 0–3, especially MBSE, Software Systems Engineering, System Design & Integration, System Test & Evaluation, and model-based analytics

## 1. Course purpose

This course prepares a systems engineer to lead complex, software-intensive, cyber-physical development and evolution when knowledge is uncertain, operational environments change, and meaningful feedback must arrive sooner than a traditional document-and-gate cycle permits. It does not treat Agile as a software ceremony package. It treats agility as an engineered capability of the **product, engineering process, operational system, and workforce**.

The learner will design deliberate learning intervals; tailor lifecycle patterns and controls; improve end-to-end flow; define common-mission, self-managing teams; architect products and engineering environments for affordable change; integrate models and digital evidence; build quality, safety, security, and verification into increments; design a cyber-physical DevSecOps and continual-integration system; and create evidence-based governance that can scale across teams, suppliers, and long-lead dependencies.

The final product is a set of systems-engineering-specific recommendations to the CEO of a fictional company, preserving the source JHU course goal. ([2])

## Week 1 — Distinguish agile systems engineering from software rituals and frame the transformation problem

### Competency alignment

C1 (D), C10 (A), C12 (A)

### Professional context and essential question

The first leadership error in an agile transformation is selecting ceremonies before defining the uncertainty, response need, mission, constraints, and evidence problem. The essential question is: **What must become more responsive, to which changes, within what response time, and without sacrificing which obligations?**

### Weekly learning outcomes

* Distinguish agility, Agile values, Lean thinking, iterative development, incremental delivery, Scrum, Kanban, DevOps, and agile systems engineering.
* Identify the system, engineering process, enterprise, and operational environment that may each need a different kind of agility.
* Characterize uncertainty, environmental dynamism, consequence, coupling, and response-time needs for the NMS case.
* Construct a transformation problem statement that names outcomes and constraints rather than prescribing ceremonies.
* Explain why “doing Agile” is not sufficient evidence that a system or engineering organization is agile.

### Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

* Reconstruct the Phase 2 lifecycle and Phase 3 evidence chain from memory.
* Name three decisions that require a controlled baseline even when work is iterative.
* Distinguish an iteration, an increment, a release, a deployment, an experiment, and an operational change.
* Identify one prior-program delay caused by late feedback and one caused by technical coupling.

A learner unable to complete at least three of the four items should revisit the cited prior-course artifacts before beginning the independent assignment.

### Required study

* JHU abridged syllabus: Description, Course Topics, Course Goals, and CLOs. Extract the source course’s definition of the systems engineer’s role and the semester-long CEO-advice objective. ([2])
* INCOSE Agile Systems Engineering primer, pp. 3–14. Study the eight strategic aspects and the distinction between a “what” and a context-specific “how.” ([5])
* Agile Manifesto and principles. Mark which statements transfer directly to multi-domain systems and which require tailoring. ([7])
* Highsmith, *Agile Project Management*, introduction and the discussion of creating innovative products under uncertainty. Record edition and chapter titles used. ([3])

For every source, record one claim, one limitation, one implication for NMS, and one question that remains unresolved.

### Instructor-style lesson notes

* Agility is the sustainable ability to sense relevant change, decide, and respond effectively within an acceptable time and cost. It is not synonymous with speed.
* Systems engineering agility can apply to the engineered product, the engineering process, the operational system, and the workforce. These are related but not identical.
* Complexity does not automatically justify less planning. It changes planning from prediction-only to hypothesis, feedback, option preservation, and rolling commitment.
* Agile values do not eliminate technical baselines. They change when and how commitment is made and how evidence updates the baseline.
* An agile transformation should begin with response requirements: stimulus, affected entity, environment, expected response, response time, cost, and effectiveness.
* The NMS case contains both reversible decisions and high-consequence commitments. The course will deliberately treat them differently.

### Worked example

NMS initially states, “Adopt Scrum across the program.” The revised problem statement is: “Reduce the elapsed time from discovery of an operational, safety, or stakeholder issue to a verified and authorized system response from a median of 120 days to 45 days, while maintaining safety-case integrity, cybersecurity controls, and configuration traceability.” The example then separates software configuration changes, hardware interface changes, supplier changes, and operational-policy changes because each has a different feasible response envelope.

Reproduce the example with the supplied case data, then change one assumption and explain the effect on the conclusion.

### Guided practice

* Classify 15 case statements as value, uncertainty, constraint, risk, method, symptom, or outcome.
* Create a four-layer agility map covering product, process, operations, and workforce.
* Write response requirements for three stimuli: cybersecurity disclosure, accessibility change, and supplier delay.
* Challenge each response requirement for feasibility and unintended incentive.

Checkpoint: compare the guided result with the reference rationale before beginning independent synthesis. Record discrepancies and corrections.

### Independent exercises

* **Foundation:** Create a two-page glossary that distinguishes the major agile/Lean terms used in the course.
* **Application:** Build an NMS uncertainty and dynamism register with at least 15 entries, including source, affected decisions, time horizon, consequence, and learnability.
* **Analysis:** Compare a ceremony-first transformation proposal with an outcome-first proposal and identify at least eight failure modes.
* **Synthesis:** Write a 1,200-word executive essay advising the CEO what agile systems engineering is and is not for this program.
* **Stretch:** Define quantitative response measures for three classes of change and explain where faster response would be unsafe or uneconomic.

### Weekly deliverable

**Agile SE Receiving and Framing Package** containing the transformation problem statement, four-layer agility map, uncertainty register, response requirements, constraints, initial assumptions, and executive essay. Audience: NMS CEO and chief engineer. Length: 8–12 pages plus tables.

## Week 2 — Assess project characteristics and tailor the lifecycle, cadence, and controls

### Competency alignment

C1 (D), C9 (D), C10 (A), C12 (D)

### Professional context and essential question

Agile methods are not equally suitable for every work item, subsystem, contract, or lifecycle commitment. The essential question is: **Which characteristics govern the degree and form of agility, and what must be tailored rather than copied?**

### Weekly learning outcomes

* Assess uncertainty, novelty, modularity, integration frequency, criticality, compliance, supplier structure, and hardware lead time.
* Differentiate reversible from hard-to-reverse decisions and align commitment timing accordingly.
* Compare sequential, iterative, incremental, spiral, flow-based, and hybrid patterns for portions of one program.
* Create a tailoring matrix that preserves required controls while shortening feedback.
* Defend where agile methods are unsuitable, premature, or limited.

### Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

* Restate the Week 1 response requirements without consulting the document.
* Classify five Phase 2 decisions as reversible, expensive to reverse, or effectively irreversible.
* Identify which system interfaces were responsible for the most integration risk.
* Recall the difference between uncertainty that can be reduced through learning and variability that must be managed operationally.

A learner unable to complete at least three of the four items should revisit the cited prior-course artifacts before beginning the independent assignment.

### Required study

* GAO *Agile Assessment Guide*, Executive Summary and chapters on Agile adoption, team/program context, and execution controls. Extract suitability and governance practices rather than treating the guide as a compliance checklist. ([6])
* NASA, “Agile Software Engineering in NASA’s Waterfall World.” Focus on incremental knowledge growth, systems-engineering interfaces, and integration with governing lifecycle expectations. ([12])
* INCOSE primer, pp. 14–19, on context dependence, long-lead dependencies, technical oversight, stakeholder engagement, and continual integration. ([5])
* DoD Systems Engineering Guidebook sections on lifecycle tailoring, technical planning, risk, and reviews. ([18])

For every source, record one claim, one limitation, one implication for NMS, and one question that remains unresolved.

### Instructor-style lesson notes

* Project characteristics govern the viable operating model. Important variables include consequence of error, rate of external change, ability to modularize, feedback latency, asset cost, and regulatory evidence.
* Tailoring is a documented engineering decision. It identifies what is retained, modified, automated, combined, moved earlier, made event-driven, or removed, and why.
* High-consequence work benefits from short evidence loops but may require stronger independence, configuration control, and authorization—not weaker rigor.
* Hardware and suppliers do not prevent agility. They change the feasible increment, simulation strategy, interface discipline, option strategy, and commitment horizon.
* A single program may use flow for defects, timeboxed learning for uncertain design, sequential qualification for a physical item, and staged operational deployment.
* The result should be a portfolio of lifecycle patterns tied to work characteristics, not one framework imposed everywhere.

### Worked example

The autonomy perception algorithm can be updated in two-week learning loops using recorded data and simulation, while a battery enclosure tooling decision requires staged commitment because tooling is expensive and the supplier lead time is 24 weeks. An interface emulator and mass/thermal surrogate allow early integration evidence before hardware arrival. The lifecycle therefore mixes frequent software increments, monthly integrated demonstrations, and controlled hardware decision gates.

Reproduce the example with the supplied case data, then change one assumption and explain the effect on the conclusion.

### Guided practice

* Score six NMS workstreams against uncertainty, feedback cost, criticality, coupling, lead time, and evidence burden.
* Plot each workstream on a reversibility-versus-consequence matrix.
* Select a lifecycle/cadence pattern for each workstream.
* Write tailoring decisions for one safety-critical workstream and one low-consequence internal tool.

Checkpoint: compare the guided result with the reference rationale before beginning independent synthesis. Record discrepancies and corrections.

### Independent exercises

* **Foundation:** Compare six lifecycle and flow patterns in a decision table.
* **Application:** Produce a project-characteristics profile for at least eight NMS workstreams.
* **Analysis:** Identify where the source organization’s current plan has either overcontrolled low-risk learning or undercontrolled high-consequence change.
* **Synthesis:** Create a tailored integrated lifecycle with event-driven reviews, learning cadences, release cadences, and authorization gates.
* **Stretch:** Perform a sensitivity analysis showing how the lifecycle changes if modularity, supplier lead time, or regulatory independence improves.

### Weekly deliverable

**Agile Suitability and Tailoring Review Package** with characteristics matrix, reversibility analysis, lifecycle pattern map, tailored controls, rejected alternatives, and a 12-slide review briefing.

Submit controlled source files, data/calculations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review against the rubric. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

### Analytic rubric

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Project-characteristic analysis | 25% |
| Lifecycle/cadence fit | 25% |
| Control tailoring and rationale | 20% |
| Risk and reversibility reasoning | 15% |
| Review quality | 15% |

Minimum weekly performance is 80%, with no critical failure.

### Critical failures

* Applying one cadence or framework to all work.
* Removing a required assurance or configuration control without replacement evidence.
* Ignoring long-lead hardware, suppliers, or integration dependencies.
* Claiming a practice is agile solely because it is iterative.

### Knowledge check and answer guidance

1. **What does project-characteristics-govern mean?**  
   **Answer guidance:** The context determines which agile/Lean strategies and controls are appropriate; method selection follows the engineering characteristics.
2. **Why separate release cadence from iteration cadence?**  
   **Answer guidance:** Learning and build cycles can occur more frequently than authorized release or operational deployment.
3. **What is tailoring?**  
   **Answer guidance:** A documented, risk-aware adaptation of lifecycle processes and controls to context, with rationale and evidence responsibilities.
4. **Why is reversibility important?**  
   **Answer guidance:** It informs how long options can remain open and how much evidence is needed before commitment.
5. **Does long-lead hardware make agile SE impossible?**  
   **Answer guidance:** No; it requires architecture, surrogates, early interfaces, staged commitments, and cadences appropriate to the dependency.

## Week 3 — Use Lean flow, queues, WIP, batch size, and value-stream evidence to shorten learning

### Competency alignment

C8 (D), C9 (D), C10 (A), C12 (D)

### Professional context and essential question

Busy teams can still deliver slowly when work waits in queues, crosses many handoffs, or is integrated in large batches. The essential question is: **Where does engineering work actually wait, why, and which flow intervention improves end-to-end learning rather than local utilization?**

### Weekly learning outcomes

* Construct current- and future-state engineering value-stream maps.
* Measure lead time, touch time, queue time, WIP, throughput, blocked time, rework, and feedback age.
* Explain Little’s Law and the effects of WIP and batch size without treating them as universal optimization formulas.
* Identify policy, approval, information, tool, supplier, and integration constraints that create queues.
* Design a bounded flow experiment with expected outcomes and guardrails.

### Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

* List the tailored cadences established in Week 2.
* Identify two workstreams where local utilization currently conflicts with system flow.
* Recall one Phase 2 defect that aged while waiting for cross-disciplinary review.
* Define a customer for an internal engineering value stream.

A learner unable to complete at least three of the four items should revisit the cited prior-course artifacts before beginning the independent assignment.

### Required study

* Lean Enterprise Institute value-stream mapping overview and guidance. Identify material, information, decision, and evidence flows. ([10])
* The Kanban Guide (May 2025), complete short guide. Focus on definition of workflow, control of WIP, service-level expectation, and flow metrics. ([9])
* GAO Agile Assessment Guide sections on work visibility, program monitoring, metrics, and continuous integration. ([6])
* Highsmith sections addressing adaptive flow, short feedback, and delivery of value. ([3])

For every source, record one claim, one limitation, one implication for NMS, and one question that remains unresolved.

### Instructor-style lesson notes

* A value stream begins with a need or question and ends when usable value or evidence reaches the decision-maker or operator. Departmental process maps often stop too early.
* Lead time includes waiting. Touch time measures active work. Improving utilization at one specialty can increase queues and total lead time.
* Little’s Law relates average WIP, throughput, and cycle time under stable assumptions. It is a diagnostic relationship, not permission to ignore variability or priority classes.
* Large batches hide defects, delay integration, and increase the cost of changed assumptions. Very small batches can create excessive transaction cost; the optimum is contextual.
* WIP limits expose constraints. They do not solve them automatically.
* Measures must include value/evidence age and quality, not just item count.

The target is 18 days lead time with no increase in escaped interface defects.

## Week 5 — Plan to discover through learning intervals, hypotheses, options, and rolling commitment

### Competency alignment

C2 (D), C8 (D), C9 (A), C10 (A)

### Professional context and essential question

Traditional plans often disguise uncertain assumptions as fixed tasks. Agile planning makes learning explicit while still controlling commitments. The essential question is: **What must be learned next, what evidence is sufficient, and which commitment should that learning enable or prevent?**

### Weekly learning outcomes

* Write falsifiable technical, operational, stakeholder, and process hypotheses.
* Design learning intervals that include build, integrate, evaluate, decide, and baseline update.
* Distinguish minimum viable product, minimum viable capability, minimum viable experiment, and minimum viable evidence.
* Create rolling-wave plans that preserve options while exposing schedule, cost, and long-lead commitments.

### Required study

* INCOSE primer, pp. 6–9, Iterative Incremental Development, Attentive Situational Awareness, and Attentive Decision Making. ([5])
* NASA Agile TIM manuscript overview and examples of hypothesis-driven, incremental engineering and model-based/embedded-system learning. ([11])
* GAO Agile Assessment Guide sections on roadmaps, release planning, requirements prioritization, and monitoring. ([6])

For every source, record one claim, one limitation, one implication for NMS, and one question that remains unresolved.

### Instructor-style lesson notes

* A learning interval is a bounded cycle from question to evidence to decision, not simply a sprint label.
* Hypotheses should predict observable behavior and define what result would change the plan.
* Minimum viable evidence is the least credible evidence needed for a specific decision—not the least work the team can demonstrate.
* Options have value when uncertainty is high and commitment is expensive, but maintaining every option creates cost and integration complexity.
* Rolling-wave plans increase detail as uncertainty falls. They do not excuse invisible dependencies or unbudgeted long-lead work.
* Each interval needs entry conditions, question, planned increment/experiment, integration target, evidence, decision owner, exit criteria, and baseline disposition.

The decision is not “finish prototype”; it is whether to retain concept A, switch to B, or defer tooling. Minimum viable evidence includes representative participants, defined measures, uncertainty bounds, safety observations, and trace to the decision criteria.

### Instructor-style lesson notes
