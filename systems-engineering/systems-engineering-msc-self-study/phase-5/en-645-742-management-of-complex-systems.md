# EN.645.742 — Management of Complex Systems

**Credits:** 3

Traditional systems engineering is usually applied to closed, precise, and recursive systems with the assertion that the methodologies used can be scaled up to more elaborate systems of systems. This course addresses the more realistic and emerging field of complex systems, where multiple current development efforts with disparate and nonlinear attributes characterize the system components. Managing complex systems must account for the likelihood of multiple disciplines, differing scales, often unpredictable future states, irreducible uncertainty, and nonlinear behavior. Customers, corporations, governments, technologies, and systems now must be considered on a global scale with a mix of new and legacy systems. The student will be encouraged to think differently and creatively about the approaches to managing complex systems and to use adaptive strategies and tools. Special attention will be given to risk assessment and management for dynamic systems. Case studies and examples will be drawn from commercial industry and DoD/government systems. Students will be expected to discuss several readings and complete academic papers to explore in depth one or more of the concepts discussed.
Prerequisite(s): EN.645.769 System Test and Evaluation or EN.655.769 Healthcare System Test and Evaluation or advisor and instructor approval.

This course is different from classic systems engineering survey courses. SEBoK distinguishes **complexity** from merely “complicated” systems and notes that complexity rises when system behavior is hard to predict and when adaptation and nonlinear interactions matter. SEBoK also has dedicated material on **systems of systems and complexity**, and INCOSE’s Complex Systems Working Group explicitly focuses on systems “beyond those for which traditional systems engineering approaches and methods were developed.” ([SEBoK][221])

### How to use this plan

Use one running case throughout all 12 weeks. Pick something with real interdependence, uncertainty, and mixed stakeholders:

* national electric grid modernization
* global supply chain resilience network
* autonomous transportation ecosystem
* military command-and-control system of systems
* smart-city emergency response network
* healthcare delivery ecosystem with insurers, providers, and digital platforms

Plan on about **8–10 hours per week**:

* 3 hours reading
* 2 hours lecture/video/resources
* 3 hours exercises
* 1–2 hours reflection and writing

Because the catalog says students are expected to discuss readings and write papers, this plan includes **three short papers** and a **final synthesis paper**.

### Week 1 — What makes a system complex?

**Goal**
Separate “complex” from merely large, technical, or difficult.

SEBoK defines complexity as difficulty in understanding behavior or predicting consequences of change, especially where there is no simple relationship between element behavior and whole-system behavior. ([SEBoK][221])

**Read**

* SEBoK: **Complexity**. ([SEBoK][221])
* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems and Complexity**. ([SEBoK][222])
* INCOSE Complex Systems Working Group overview. ([INCOSE][223])

**Exercises**

1. Write a 1-page memo: “Why my chosen case is complex, not just complicated.”
2. Identify:

   * interacting subsystems
   * major stakeholder groups
   * uncertainties
   * nonlinear interactions
   * legacy constraints
3. List 5 examples of emergent behavior that could occur.

**Deliverable**

* Complexity framing memo
* Case study definition

---

### Week 2 — Traditional SE vs. complex-systems management

**Goal**
Understand where classical systems engineering fits, and where it breaks down.

SEBoK notes that SoS engineering applies technical management across mixtures of existing and new systems, while the complexity material emphasizes that traditional methods need adaptation when systems are loosely coupled, evolving, and independently managed. ([SEBoK][202])

**Read**

* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems (SoS)**. ([SEBoK][202])
* SEBoK: **Systems Engineering Overview** for the baseline traditional framing. ([SEBoK][185])
* INCOSE resource: **System Engineering Heuristics for Complex Systems**. ([INCOSE][224])

**Exercises**

1. Compare traditional SE and complex-systems management across:

   * boundary clarity
   * requirements stability
   * control authority
   * predictability
   * lifecycle structure
   * testability
2. For your case, identify what can still be managed conventionally and what needs adaptive approaches.

**Deliverable**

* 2-page comparison chart

---

### Week 3 — Interdependence, emergence, and nonlinear behavior

**Goal**
Learn to reason about feedback loops and unintended consequences.

SEBoK’s complexity and SoS material both emphasize that whole-system behavior can emerge from interactions and may not be reducible to individual subsystem behavior. ([SEBoK][221])

**Read**

* Re-read SEBoK **Complexity** and **Systems of Systems and Complexity**. ([SEBoK][221])
* INCOSE **Systems of Systems Complexity** presentation for a useful structural/functional lens. ([INCOSE][225])

**Exercises**

1. Draw a causal loop or influence diagram for your case.
2. Identify:

   * reinforcing feedback loops
   * balancing loops
   * delay effects
   * threshold effects
3. Write three “if this changes, what else shifts?” chains.

**Deliverable**

* Feedback map
* Emergence notes

---

### Week 4 — Multiple scales, disciplines, and stakeholders

**Goal**
Handle technical, organizational, social, and policy layers together.

INCOSE’s complex-systems work explicitly sits at the intersection of complexity science and systems engineering, and SEBoK frames SoS work as spanning multiple independently managed systems and organizations. ([INCOSE][223])

**Read**

* INCOSE Complex Systems Working Group page. ([INCOSE][223])
* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems (SoS)**. ([SEBoK][202])

**Exercises**

1. Build a stakeholder and governance map showing:

   * owners/operators
   * regulators
   * suppliers
   * users
   * external influencers
2. Identify where objectives conflict.
3. Identify where scale mismatches occur:

   * local vs global
   * short-term vs long-term
   * subsystem vs enterprise

**Deliverable**

* Stakeholder/governance map

---

### Week 5 — Uncertainty, unpredictability, and adaptive strategy

**Goal**
Learn to manage under uncertainty instead of assuming it away.

The course description explicitly emphasizes unpredictable futures and irreducible uncertainty. DoD’s Risk, Issue, and Opportunity guide also stresses that programs should surface assumptions and continuously manage changing risks rather than treating risk as a one-time exercise. ([CTO][226])

**Read**

* DoD **Risk, Issue, and Opportunity Management Guide**. ([CTO][226])
* INCOSE complex-systems heuristics resource. ([INCOSE][224])

**Exercises**

1. Make an uncertainty inventory:

   * technical
   * operational
   * stakeholder/political
   * market/economic
   * regulatory
   * environmental
2. Classify each as:

   * reducible
   * partially reducible
   * irreducible
3. For each major uncertainty, choose an adaptive response:

   * monitor
   * hedge
   * prototype
   * diversify
   * defer commitment
   * build margin/flexibility

**Deliverable**

* Uncertainty register

**Paper 1**

* 2–3 pages: “How adaptive strategy differs from deterministic planning in my case.”

---

### Week 6 — Dynamic risk assessment and management

**Goal**
Treat risk as evolving, systemic, and cross-domain.

The catalog specifically says there will be special attention to risk assessment and management for dynamic systems. The DoD RIO guide describes risk management as both top-down and bottom-up and emphasizes continuous identification, analysis, mitigation, and monitoring. ([CTO][226])

**Read**

* DoD **Risk, Issue, and Opportunity Management Guide**. ([CTO][226])
* SEBoK: **System of Systems and Complexity**. ([SEBoK][222])

**Exercises**

1. Create a risk register with at least 15 entries:

   * cascading failures
   * interface instability
   * governance breakdown
   * legacy incompatibility
   * policy shifts
   * data quality issues
2. Add:

   * likelihood
   * consequence
   * detectability
   * propagation potential
   * response strategy
3. Identify 5 leading indicators for risk monitoring.

**Deliverable**

* Dynamic risk register
* Risk dashboard sketch

---

### Week 7 — Legacy systems, modernization, and evolutionary change

**Goal**
Manage systems made of old and new components.

The course description explicitly mentions the mix of new and legacy systems on a global scale. SEBoK’s SoS framing is useful here because many complex systems are federations of independently owned systems with uneven modernization timelines. ([SEBoK][202])

**Read**

* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems (SoS)**. ([SEBoK][202])
* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems and Complexity**. ([SEBoK][222])
* DoD **Engineering of Defense Systems Guidebook** overview material for modernization context. ([CTO][206])

**Exercises**

1. Identify all legacy elements in your case.
2. Assess for each:

   * replacement difficulty
   * interoperability burden
   * risk contribution
   * hidden dependencies
3. Propose an incremental modernization roadmap.

**Deliverable**

* Legacy-to-future transition plan

---

### Week 8 — Governance, decision-making, and distributed control

**Goal**
Understand how to manage systems when nobody fully controls the whole.

SEBoK notes that SoSE often involves planning and integrating across systems that remain under separate technical management. That makes governance and decision rights central, not secondary. ([SEBoK][202])

**Read**

* SEBoK: **Systems of Systems (SoS)**. ([SEBoK][202])
* SEBoK: **Systems Engineering Overview**. ([SEBoK][185])

**Exercises**

1. Map decision rights:

   * who can change what
   * who funds what
   * who bears risk
   * who sets policy
2. Identify 5 governance bottlenecks.
3. Design a lightweight governance model for coordination:

   * escalation path
   * interface boards
   * risk forum
   * change review cadence

**Deliverable**

* Governance model memo

---

### Week 9 — Measures, indicators, and resilience in complex systems

**Goal**
Decide what to measure when simple KPIs are not enough.

MITRE’s resilience metrics material is useful here because it distinguishes measures of effectiveness from lower-level indicators and offers ways to think about performance under disruption. ([CTO][206])

**Read**

* MITRE metrics/resilience material surfaced in the search results. ([CTO][206])
* SEBoK SoS/system-analysis related material through the SoS pages already used. ([SEBoK][202])

**Exercises**

1. Define 5 high-level measures of effectiveness for your system.
2. Define 10 monitoring indicators, including:

   * adaptability
   * recovery time
   * coupling stress
   * queue or backlog growth
   * mission degradation under disruption
3. Create an “early warning” dashboard concept.

**Deliverable**

* Complex-system metrics framework

**Paper 2**

* 2–3 pages: “What should leaders measure in a complex system, and what should they avoid over-trusting?”

### Week 10 — Case studies: commercial and government/DoD systems

**Goal**
Learn from real-world patterns of failure, adaptation, and recovery.

The course description explicitly says case studies will be drawn from commercial industry and DoD/government systems. DoD’s engineering and risk guides are useful reference points for the government side, while SEBoK’s SoS framing translates well to commercial platforms and infrastructure ecosystems. ([CTO][206])

**Read**

* DoD **Engineering of Defense Systems Guidebook**. ([CTO][206])
* DoD **Risk, Issue, and Opportunity Management Guide**. ([CTO][226])
* SEBoK SoS pages. ([SEBoK][202])

**Exercises**

1. Pick two cases:

   * one commercial
   * one government/DoD
2. For each, analyze:

   * sources of complexity
   * stakeholders and governance
   * nonlinear effects
   * risk signals
   * adaptive responses
3. Compare them to your running case.

**Deliverable**

* Two case study briefs

---

### Week 11 — Managing interventions and unintended consequences

**Goal**
Learn to change a complex system without making it worse.

SEBoK’s complexity discussion stresses that changing one part of a complex system can produce hard-to-predict whole-system effects. That makes experimental, staged intervention important. ([SEBoK][221])

**Read**

* Revisit SEBoK **Complexity**. ([SEBoK][221])
* Revisit INCOSE **System Engineering Heuristics for Complex Systems**. ([INCOSE][224])

**Exercises**

1. Propose 3 possible interventions in your case system.
2. For each, analyze:

   * intended effect
   * likely second-order effects
   * stakeholder reactions
   * reversibility
   * safe-to-fail experiment design
3. Pick one intervention and design a phased rollout.

**Deliverable**

* Intervention strategy memo

**Paper 3**

* 3 pages: “How to intervene in a complex system without assuming full predictability.”

---

### Week 12 — Final synthesis: an adaptive management framework

**Goal**
Pull the whole course together into one coherent management approach.

**Exercises**

1. Build a final framework for your case including:

   * system definition and complexity sources
   * stakeholder/governance map
   * uncertainty inventory
   * dynamic risk register
   * resilience indicators
   * adaptive intervention strategy
   * modernization roadmap
2. Write a **5–7 page final paper**:

   * why the system is complex
   * why traditional management alone is insufficient
   * what adaptive strategies you recommend
   * what must be monitored continuously
   * what risks are irreducible
   * how leadership should make decisions under uncertainty

**Deliverable**

* Final synthesis paper
* Final presentation outline

### Best resource stack for this course

These are the strongest anchors for the full 12 weeks:

* **SEBoK v2.13**, which was updated in November 2025 and includes current entries on **Complexity**, **Systems of Systems**, and **Systems of Systems and Complexity**. ([SEBoK][183])
* **INCOSE Complex Systems Working Group**, which focuses specifically on systems beyond the scope of traditional methods. ([INCOSE][223])
* **INCOSE System Engineering Heuristics for Complex Systems**, which gives practical heuristics for engineering and managing complexity. ([INCOSE][224])
* **DoD Risk, Issue, and Opportunity Management Guide**, especially for dynamic and evolving risk management. ([CTO][226])
* **DoD Engineering of Defense Systems Guidebook** for large-scale defense and government program context. ([CTO][206])

### What you should be able to do after 12 weeks

By the end, you should be able to:

* explain why complex systems require different management approaches
* distinguish complexity, complication, and systems-of-systems issues
* identify nonlinear interactions and emergent behavior
* manage uncertainty without pretending it can all be eliminated
* build adaptive risk-management and governance structures
* plan modernization across legacy and new systems
* write thoughtful, graduate-level papers on complex-system management
